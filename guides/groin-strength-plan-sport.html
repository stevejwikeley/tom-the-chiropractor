Getting Your Groin Strong Again, For Sport

A staged rehab plan for long-standing adductor tendon pain

Written by Tom Wikeley, chiropractor, registered with the General Chiropractic Council (No. 05268). Last reviewed November 2026.

Please read this first

This is a rehab plan I give to my own patients after I have examined them, when we agree the pain is coming from the adductor tendon on the inside of the thigh. It is on the website so it is easy to find and re-read, and so anyone can see what a sensible plan looks like. It is general information, not a diagnosis, and not a treatment plan for anybody I have not assessed.

Groin pain has more possible causes than most people expect, and some of them need something other than exercise. No plan can tell you which one you have. If nobody has examined you, please treat this as background reading, start with section 11, and see your GP, a chiropractor, a physiotherapist or another qualified clinician about your own symptoms. If a clinician who has examined you has given you different advice, follow theirs, not this.

There is a version of this plan for everyday life rather than sport. If you are not going back to a sport, read Getting Your Groin Strong Again, For Everyday Life instead.

Some symptoms need urgent care, not reading

Go to A&E, or call 999, if you have any of these, whatever else this guide says:

  • Sudden, severe groin or hip pain and you cannot put weight on the leg, especially if you are a runner who has been building up mileage. This can be a stress fracture of the hip and needs checking the same day
  • Groin pain with a fever, or feeling generally very unwell
  • Sudden, severe pain in a testicle, or swelling of the testicle
  • A painful lump in your groin that will not go back in, especially with sickness or tummy pain

Section 11 has the full list, including what to take to your GP or to tell me about.

Your groin has been sore for a while now. It flares when you kick, sprint, change direction or get out of the car, it settles a bit with rest, and then it comes straight back the moment you push it again.

That on and off pattern is really common with this problem, and it tells us what to do.

Rest has not fixed it, and on its own it will not. What fixes it is making the tendon and the muscles around your hip strong enough for what you ask of them. That takes a plan, done steadily, for about three months.

This plan assumes I have examined you and we agree the pain is coming from your adductor tendon. If nobody has examined you, read section 11 first.

The short version

  • Your adductor tendon is irritated and a bit under strength, not torn and not "worn out".
  • Strengthening is the treatment. In the best known trial of this problem, about 8 out of 10 people who did a strengthening programme got back to their sport without groin pain, compared with about 1 in 8 who had passive treatment.
  • One rule guides everything: pain up to 5 out of 10 is OK during exercise, as long as it has settled by the next morning.
  • There are four stages. You move up when you pass the tests at the end of each stage, not just because a certain number of weeks has gone by.
  • Expect around 12 weeks, sometimes longer. Groin problems that have been there for months are slow to turn round.
  • Keep one strength session a week going for good once you are back. It is the best thing you can do to stop it coming back.

1. What is actually going on

Your adductors are the muscles on the inside of your thigh. They pull your legs together, help you change direction, and do a lot of the work when you kick or push off sideways.

They join onto the front of your pelvis, near the middle, by a short, tough tendon. That attachment point is where your pain is.

Tendons are living tissue. They get stronger when you load them and weaker when you do not. When the demand on the tendon, from sport, running, kicking or twisting, is more than it can currently handle, it gets irritated and sore.

When this has been going on for a few months, it is not really an "inflamed" tendon any more. It is a tendon that has become sensitive and a bit less able to take load. That is good news, because sensitivity calms down and capacity can be built.

Two things are worth knowing:

  • Pain does not mean damage. A sore tendon is not a tendon that is tearing. Some pain while you rebuild is normal and safe.
  • The muscles around it matter. Weak adductors, weak trunk muscles and poor control around the hip all put more strain on that one attachment. The plan works on all of them.

2. The one rule

During exercise, pain up to about 5 out of 10 is fine. By the next morning it should be back to your normal. And it should not be creeping up week by week.

  • Stayed under 5 and settled by morning: you got it right. Carry on.
  • Went above 5, or still sore next morning: you did too much. Not harm, just too much. Repeat the last session's level rather than moving on.
  • Slowly getting worse over two or three weeks: drop back a stage and tell me.

Your morning check is your best guide. Each morning, do the squeeze test in section 3 and give it a score out of 10. It tells you far more than how you felt during yesterday's session.

3. Your starting tests

Do these before you start and then once a week. Write the numbers down. Pain scores bounce around from day to day, so these numbers are how you will see real progress.

The squeeze test. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat. Put a football, or your fist, between your knees. Squeeze as hard as you can for 3 seconds. Score the pain out of 10. In clinic I may also measure how hard you can squeeze.

The side plank adductor hold. Lie on your side, propped up on your elbow. Rest the inside of your top knee on a bench or sofa seat, with the bottom leg hanging. Lift your hips so your body is in a straight line. Time how long you can hold before pain goes over 5 out of 10 or your form goes. Do both sides. The difference between your good side and your sore side is the number that matters. If this is too painful to try at the start, just write "too painful". That is fine and it is common.

Your sport check. Write down what you currently cannot do because of your groin. For example, "cannot kick a long ball", "groin aches after 20 minutes of five-a-side", "sore getting out of the car".

4. Stage 1: Settle it down

Roughly weeks 1 to 3

Goal: calm the tendon down and get it used to being worked again.

Just two exercises to start. Keep it simple and do them well.

Exercise How much How often
Ball squeeze hold 5 holds of 30 seconds, rest 1 minute between Every day
Glute bridge 3 sets of 12, slow Every other day

Ball squeeze hold. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat, a football or a firm pillow between your knees. Squeeze steadily, about 6 out of 10 effort, and hold for 30 seconds. Breathe normally. As it gets easier, squeeze a little harder.

Glute bridge. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat. Push through your heels and lift your hips until your body is in a straight line from shoulders to knees. Hold for 2 seconds at the top. Lower slowly. Keep the ball between your knees and squeeze it gently as you lift once it feels easy.

Keep moving. Walking, cycling and swimming, but not breaststroke, are all fine, as long as they stay within the one rule. Stop sprinting, kicking and sharp twisting for now. You will get them back.

Move up to Stage 2 when you can tick all of these

  • Squeeze test pain is 3 out of 10 or less.
  • You have done the Stage 1 exercises for at least a week with no next morning flare.
  • Everyday things like walking, stairs and getting out of the car are comfortable or nearly comfortable.

5. Stage 2: Build strength

Roughly weeks 3 to 6

Goal: start building real strength in your adductors, hips and trunk.

Strength sessions three days a week, never two days in a row. Keep the ball squeeze hold going on the days in between if it helps.

Exercise How much
Side lying adductor lift 3 sets of 12 each side, slow
Short lever Copenhagen (see section 8) Start with holds: 4 holds of 15 seconds each side. Then move to 3 sets of 6 to 8 lifts
Split squat 3 sets of 10 each leg
Side plank, on your knees or feet 3 holds of 20 to 30 seconds each side

Side lying adductor lift. Lie on your sore side. Bend your top leg and put that foot on the floor in front of you. Keep your bottom leg straight. Lift the bottom leg a hand's width off the floor, hold for 2 seconds, lower slowly over 3 seconds. Add an ankle weight when 3 sets of 12 feels easy.

Split squat. Stand with one foot well in front of the other. Lower your back knee towards the floor, keeping your body upright, then push back up. Hold onto something for balance if you need to.

Work both sides. Always do the exercises on your good side too.

Move up to Stage 3 when you can tick all of these

  • Squeeze test pain is 2 out of 10 or less.
  • You can do 3 sets of 8 short lever Copenhagen lifts on your sore side, within the one rule.
  • No next morning flare for at least a week.
  • Brisk walking, cycling and light jogging in a straight line feel fine.

6. Stage 3: Get heavy

Roughly weeks 6 to 10

Goal: make you properly strong, on one leg, in the positions your sport puts you in.

Three strength days a week. On two other days, start the running plan in section 9.

Exercise How much
Long lever Copenhagen (see section 8) Build from holds of 15 to 20 seconds to 3 sets of 6 to 10 lifts each side
Side lunge 3 sets of 8 each side. Add weight when easy
Wide stance squat, feet wider than shoulders, toes turned out 3 sets of 8 to 10. Add weight when easy
Sliding side lunge, towel or paper plate under one foot on a smooth floor 3 sets of 10 each side
Single leg balance with reach 3 sets of 8 reaches each leg

Side lunge. Stand with feet wide. Shift your weight to one side, bending that knee and sitting your hips back, keeping the other leg straight. Push back to the middle.

Sliding side lunge. Stand with a towel under one foot. Slide that foot out to the side as you bend the other knee, then pull it back in using your inner thigh. The pulling back in is the important bit.

Single leg balance with reach. Stand on one leg. Reach your other foot out in front, out to the side and behind you, touching the floor lightly each time, without losing balance.

Adding weight. The reps should come down and the weight go up as you get stronger. If nothing is getting heavier, you are not progressing. Dumbbells, a kettlebell or a loaded rucksack all work.

Move up to Stage 4 when you can tick all of these

  • Squeeze test is pain free, or 1 out of 10 at most, at full effort.
  • You can do 3 sets of 8 long lever Copenhagen lifts on each side, and your sore side is close to your good side, within about 1 or 2 reps, or a few seconds on the hold.
  • You can jog for 20 minutes without it flaring the next morning.
  • Side lunges with weight feel solid and pain free.

7. Stage 4: Back to your sport

Roughly weeks 9 to 12 and beyond

Goal: get your groin used to speed, changes of direction and kicking again.

Keep two strength sessions a week going, long lever Copenhagen plus two other Stage 3 exercises. On other days, build up your sport movements in this order. Only move to the next line when the one before feels fine and you pass the one rule.

Step What to do
1 Faster running in a straight line, building up to about 80 per cent of top speed
2 Side shuffles and backwards running
3 Gentle curves and wide turns while running
4 Sharp changes of direction at half speed, then three quarter speed, then full
5 Short passes with the inside of your foot, building to longer passes
6 Shooting and long kicks, starting at half power
7 Full training with your team or club
8 Matches, starting with part of a game if you can

Kicking is usually the last thing to come back. Long kicks and shots put more load through this tendon than almost anything else. Do not rush them.

Ready to return to full sport when you can tick all of these

  • Squeeze test is pain free at full effort.
  • Long lever Copenhagen on your sore side is about the same as your good side.
  • You have done full speed running, cutting and kicking with pain no more than 2 out of 10, settled by next morning.
  • You have done at least one or two full training sessions with no next day flare.
  • You feel confident. If you are still worried about it, you are probably not quite ready, and that is worth a chat.

8. The Copenhagen ladder

This is the most important exercise in the plan. It is hard, it works the inner thigh muscles very strongly, and it is the one exercise that has been shown to cut groin problems in footballers. Climb it one rung at a time.

Rung Position Do this
1 Short lever hold. Inside of top knee on the bench 4 holds of 15 seconds
2 Short lever lifts. Same position, lower your hips to the floor and lift back up 3 sets of 6 to 8, slow
3 Long lever hold. Inside of top ankle on the bench, top leg straight 4 holds of 15 to 20 seconds
4 Long lever lifts. Same position, lower and lift 3 sets of 6 to 10, slow
5 Long lever lifts with the bottom leg moving. Bring the bottom knee up to meet the bench as you lift 3 sets of 6 to 10

How to set up. Lie on your side, propped on your elbow, elbow under your shoulder. Put the top leg on a bench, sofa seat or low chair. Lift your hips until your body is straight. Keep your body in a straight line. Do not let your hips sag back or twist.

Move up a rung when you can do the full amount for the rung you are on, staying within the one rule, two sessions in a row.

If it hurts too much on your sore side, do the rung below on that side and the harder rung on your good side. That is fine.

9. Running plan

Start this in Stage 3, on days you are not doing strength work. Straight lines only at first.

Week Run
1 1 minute jog, 1 minute walk, repeat 8 times
2 2 minutes jog, 1 minute walk, repeat 6 times
3 4 minutes jog, 1 minute walk, repeat 4 times
4 20 minutes easy continuous jog
5 onwards Build up time and speed, then move into Stage 4

Soft, flat ground is easier than hills or rough ground. Change one thing at a time: go further, or faster, or on harder ground, not all three in the same week.

10. What to expect

Weeks 1 to 3. Pain may not change much. Your squeeze test score and morning stiffness are usually the first things to improve. This is where most people give up. Keep going.

Weeks 4 to 8. You should be clearly stronger. Everyday things should be much easier. Running starts to feel normal.

Weeks 8 to 12. Sport movements come back, kicking last.

Some people take longer than 12 weeks, especially if the pain has been there for a year or more. That does not mean it is not working. If you are getting stronger, you are on the right track.

Once you are back, keep the Copenhagen going once or twice a week. In footballers, doing just this one exercise regularly cut groin problems by about 40 per cent. The people whose groin pain comes back are usually the ones who stop the moment it stops hurting.

Stretching on its own has not been shown to fix this. A gentle stretch is fine if it feels good, but it is not a replacement for the strength work.

11. When to get help

Most groin pain in active people comes from the adductors, the hip or the lower tummy muscles. A few things need checking.

Go to A&E

  • Sudden, severe groin or hip pain and you cannot put weight on the leg, especially if you are a runner who has been building up mileage. This can be a stress fracture of the hip and needs checking the same day.
  • Groin pain with a fever, or feeling generally very unwell.
  • Sudden, severe pain in a testicle, or swelling of the testicle. This needs urgent assessment.
  • A painful lump in your groin that will not go back in, especially with sickness or tummy pain.

Contact your GP or tell me

  • A lump or bulge in your groin that appears when you cough, strain or stand, and goes away when you lie down. This could be a hernia.
  • Deep pain in the front of the hip, clicking or catching, or pain when you sit for a long time or bend your hip up towards your chest. This can point to the hip joint itself rather than the tendon.
  • Groin pain that is there at night or at rest and does not change with movement.
  • Pain when you cough or sneeze, or pain low in your tummy.
  • Unexplained weight loss, or problems passing urine.
  • Numbness or pins and needles in your inner thigh.
  • Pain that is getting steadily worse over several weeks even though you are following this plan carefully.

Everything else, including a sore day after a hard session, can wait for your normal appointment.

12. If you want to read or watch more

Practical

  • Tame the Beast. A five minute animation by Professor Lorimer Moseley on how pain works. tamethebeast.org
  • Recovery Strategies, by Greg Lehman. A free illustrated workbook on load, pacing and pain. greglehman.ca/recovery-strategies-pain-guidebook

The research behind this plan

  • Hölmich P et al. Effectiveness of active physical training as treatment for long-standing adductor-related groin pain in athletes: randomised trial. The Lancet, 1999. The main trial behind the strengthening approach and the 8 to 12 week timeframe.
  • Weir A et al. Manual or exercise therapy for long-standing adductor-related groin pain: a randomised controlled clinical trial. Manual Therapy, 2011. Why I am honest that some people take longer than 12 weeks.
  • Harøy J et al. The Adductor Strengthening Programme prevents groin problems among male football players: a cluster-randomised controlled trial. British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2019. The Copenhagen exercise in section 8 and the advice to keep it going.
  • Weir A et al. Doha agreement meeting on terminology and definitions in groin pain in athletes. British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2015.
  • Silbernagel KG et al. Continued sports activity, using a pain monitoring model, during rehabilitation in patients with Achilles tendinopathy. American Journal of Sports Medicine, 2007. The one rule in section 2.
