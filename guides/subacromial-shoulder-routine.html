Your 8 Week Subacromial Shoulder Routine

For the early and mid stages of rotator cuff pain

Written by Tom Wikeley, chiropractor, registered with the General Chiropractic Council (No. 05268). Last reviewed November 2026.

Please read this first

This is a loading routine I give to my own patients after I have examined them, for the common kind of shoulder pain that comes from the rotator cuff tendons being asked to do more than they were ready for. It is on the website so it is easy to find and re-read, and so anyone can see what a sensible routine looks like. It is general information, not a diagnosis, and not a treatment plan for anybody I have not assessed.

Shoulder pain has more possible causes than most people expect, and a few of them have nothing to do with the shoulder at all. No routine can tell you which one you have. If nobody has examined you, please treat this as background reading and see your GP, a chiropractor, a physiotherapist or another qualified clinician about your own symptoms. If a clinician who has examined you has given you different advice, follow theirs, not this.

Some symptoms need urgent care, not reading

Call 999 now if you have any of these, whatever else this guide says:

  • Shoulder or arm pain with chest tightness or pressure, breathlessness, sweating, feeling sick, or light headedness. Heart problems can be felt in the left shoulder, arm or jaw, sometimes with no chest pain at all. Pain that comes on with effort and eases with rest is a particular worry
  • Sudden severe pain at the tip of the shoulder along with tummy pain, especially after an injury

Contact your GP or NHS 111 today if you have a red, hot, swollen shoulder with a fever, a sudden inability to lift or rotate the arm at all after a fall, an obviously deformed shoulder, new or worsening numbness or weakness in the arm or hand, unexplained weight loss or night sweats, a past history of cancer, a new lump around the shoulder, in the armpit or above the collarbone, or a cough that will not settle.

Subacromial pain is the most common sort of shoulder pain. It is felt at the top and outside of the shoulder, often in a patch around the top of the arm, and it is worse reaching up, reaching behind you, and lying on that side at night. You may also hear it called rotator cuff pain, or impingement. They describe much the same problem.

The useful thing to know is that it is usually a loading problem rather than a damaged joint. The tendons have been asked to do more than they were ready for, by a new job, a weekend of decorating, a change at the gym, or simply by years of the same movement. Tendons respond to load. Resting a sore shoulder completely tends to make it weaker and, in the end, more painful. The whole point of this routine is to put load back through the shoulder at a level it can cope with, and then to raise that level slowly.

Eight weeks is the honest minimum, and shoulders are slower than necks and backs. Three months is a perfectly normal timescale for one of these to settle properly. You should notice something well before the end, but keep going after you do.

Which stage are you in

The routine has two blocks of four weeks, but the week numbers are only a guide. What actually decides it is how your shoulder behaves.

  • Early stage. The shoulder is still sharp. It catches when you reach up, it aches at rest, and it wakes you at night. Anything that moves the joint under load flares it. The work here is holds that do not move the joint at all.
  • Mid stage. The sharpness has gone out of it. You can lift the arm in front of you without a catch, and the night pain is easing. Now the shoulder is ready for load that moves.

Move on to weeks 5 to 8 when you can raise your arm in front of you to shoulder height without a sharp catch, and the exercises you are already doing are not leaving you sore the next morning. For some people that happens in two weeks. For others it takes six. Going on too early is the most common way to stall this routine, and there is no prize for being quick.

How it should feel

  • Some pain while you work is expected, and it is not a sign that you are doing damage.
  • A useful rule: up to about 4 out of 10 is acceptable during an exercise.
  • It should settle back to your usual level within about an hour, and your shoulder should be no worse the next morning.
  • If it is worse the next morning, do fewer repetitions or use a lighter band next time rather than stopping altogether.
  • Sharp, catching or sickening pain is different. Back off from whatever caused it.

Your routine at a glance

Weeks What you do How often
1 to 4 Outward press, table slides, blade squeezes Every day
5 to 8 Those, progressed, plus band turns and band rows Daily, band work every other day

Keep doing the earlier exercises as you add the new ones.

Weeks 1 to 4

Three exercises. None of them moves the shoulder under load, which is what makes them tolerable while it is still sore.

1. Outward press

  • Stand side on to a wall with your sore shoulder nearest to it.
  • Bend that elbow to a right angle and tuck it against your ribs. A folded towel between your elbow and your side helps keep it there.
  • Press the back of your wrist into the wall, as though turning your forearm outwards. Nothing moves.
  • Press at about three quarters of your strength. Firm enough to feel the muscle working, not hard enough to sharpen the pain.
  • Hold for 20 seconds, then rest for 20 seconds.
  • 5 holds, 2 times a day.

2. Table slides

  • Sit at a table with a tea towel under the hand on your sore side.
  • Slide your hand forwards across the table, leaning your body forward so the arm rises without you lifting it.
  • Go only as far as is comfortable, and stop short of any sharp catch.
  • Hold for 3 seconds at the far end, then slide back.
  • 10 slides, 2 times a day.

3. Shoulder blade squeezes

  • Sit or stand tall with your arms relaxed by your sides.
  • Draw both shoulder blades gently back and down, as if putting them in your back pockets. Do not shrug upwards.
  • Hold for 5 seconds, then let go.
  • 10 times, 2 times a day.

Weeks 5 to 8

Keep all three of the above going, with two changes.

The outward press gets harder: hold for 30 seconds instead of 20, and let your elbow sit a few inches away from your side rather than tucked in.

The table slides come off the table: stand up, thumb pointing at the ceiling, and raise your arm in front of you as high as is comfortable. Lower it slowly, taking about 3 seconds on the way down. 10 times, 2 times a day. Once that is easy, hold a tin of beans, then a weight of 1kg to 2kg.

Then add these two. You need a resistance band, the long flat sort, which costs a few pounds.

4. Band outward turn

  • Tie the band to a door handle at about elbow height.
  • Stand side on with your sore arm furthest from the door, elbow bent to a right angle and tucked in with the towel.
  • Hold the band across your body and turn your forearm outwards, away from your stomach, about two hand widths. Your elbow stays against your side.
  • Come back slowly, taking about 3 seconds.
  • 12 repetitions, 3 sets, every other day.

5. Band rows

  • Fix the band at about chest height and face the door, holding one end in each hand with your arms out in front of you.
  • Pull both elbows back past your ribs, squeezing your shoulder blades together as you go.
  • Come back slowly, taking about 3 seconds.
  • 12 repetitions, 3 sets, every other day.

Step the band tension up when 3 sets of 12 start to feel easy, not before. Slow and heavy beats fast and light for a tendon.

Check your progress

Stand facing a wall, toes a hand's width from it. Walk your fingers up the wall as far as is comfortable and mark where they reach with a bit of tape. Do this in week 1, week 4 and week 8, always in the same spot.

Two other things worth noting down: how many nights a week the shoulder wakes you, and one everyday job that is currently awkward, such as fastening a seatbelt or getting a plate off a high shelf. Those often improve before the pain score does.

Write it down somewhere, even on the back of this page. Memory is a poor judge of slow change, and a mark you made six weeks ago is far more convincing than how you think your shoulder feels today.

Other things that help

  • Keep using the arm for everyday things below shoulder height. Protecting it completely is what makes it stiff.
  • At night, lie on the good side and hug a pillow so the sore arm is supported in front of you rather than hanging.
  • Heat for 10 to 15 minutes before your exercises if it feels stiff.
  • Stop testing it. Reaching up repeatedly to see whether it still hurts winds the shoulder up and tells you nothing the wall mark will not tell you better.
  • Keep walking and stay generally active. Sleep and general health do more for a grumpy tendon than most people credit.

Stop and contact me if you notice

  • Sudden weakness, so you cannot lift the arm at all
  • The shoulder stiffening rather than easing, particularly if turning it outwards becomes very limited
  • New pins and needles or numbness down the arm or into the hand
  • Night pain that is getting steadily worse week by week
  • Pain with a fever or feeling generally unwell

If any of these come on suddenly or feel severe, call 999 or go to A&E.

Anything else, including a few sore days, is worth mentioning at your next appointment rather than stopping the routine over.

Bring this with you when you come in, along with your wall mark. We can adjust the numbers to suit how you are getting on.
