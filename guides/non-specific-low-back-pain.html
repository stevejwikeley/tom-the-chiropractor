Understanding Non-Specific Low Back Pain

A guide to the commonest sort of back pain there is, and how pain really works

Written by Tom Wikeley, chiropractor, registered with the General Chiropractic Council (No. 05268). Last reviewed October 2026.

Please read this first

This is one of the guides I hand to my own patients after I have examined them. I have put them on the website so they are easy to find and re-read, and so anyone can learn how pain works. They are general information, not a diagnosis, and not a treatment plan for anybody I have not assessed.

No guide can tell you what is causing your pain. That takes a proper history and examination. If nobody has examined you, please treat this as background reading and see your GP, a chiropractor, a physiotherapist or another qualified clinician about your own symptoms. If a clinician who has examined you has given you different advice, follow theirs, not this.

Some symptoms need urgent care, not reading

Go straight to A&E, or call 999, if you have any of these, whatever else this guide says. Do not wait to see if they settle.

  • Numbness or a changed feeling between your legs, around your back passage or genitals, including toilet paper feeling different when you wipe
  • Trouble starting to wee, not being able to feel it coming out, or any new loss of control of your bladder or bowels
  • Numbness or pain down both legs at once
  • New or quickly worsening weakness in one or both legs, or your legs giving way
  • Sudden severe pain in your back or tummy with faintness, a cold sweat, or a throbbing feeling in your tummy

Section 13 has the full list, including what to take to your GP or NHS 111 the same day.

Back pain is one of the most common things people experience, and one of the most misunderstood. Most people assume that a sore back means something in it is damaged, worn out, or out of place. Usually none of those things is true, and believing them makes the pain harder to shift.

This guide is short on purpose. Read it in one sitting.

One thing first. It assumes I have examined you and ruled out the serious causes. If nobody has examined you yet, read section 13 first.

The short version

  • Your back pain is real. Everything below assumes that.
  • "Non-specific" does not mean unexplained. It means no single structure is injured and no serious disease is present.
  • Pain is an alarm, not a damage meter. The volume does not tell you the size of the problem.
  • Scans find wear in almost everyone, including people who have never had a day of back pain.
  • Most of the improvement happens in the first six weeks.
  • It comes back in spells for most people. That is the normal shape of this, not a failure.
  • Guarding is the thing that keeps it going. Holding yourself stiff protects you for a week and costs you after that.
  • Moving is safe. Resting is the risk.
  • Walking is the single best habit you can build.

1. What "non-specific" actually means

When I say your back pain is non-specific, it sounds like a shrug. It is not.

It means two things, and both are good news.

First, no single structure is injured. Not a slipped disc, not a trapped nerve, not a bone out of place. When I examine a back like yours, I find a back that is stiff, tender and working hard. That is not the same as a back that is damaged.

Second, no serious disease is present. That is the part of the examination that matters most, and it is the part that lets everything else in this guide apply to you.

So it is not "we do not know what is wrong". It is "nothing is broken, and the problem is in how the back is working rather than in what it is made of". That is the sort of problem that responds to what you do.

About 9 in 10 people with back pain are in this group. You are in the biggest and by far the most treatable category there is.

2. How pain works

Two things are worth knowing, because they change what you do next.

First, pain is produced by your brain, not delivered by your tissues. Its job is protection, not measurement. Your brain takes the signals coming in and weighs them against everything else it knows: how tired you are, how stressed you are, what happened last time, what you think this means. Then it decides how loud to make the alarm.

This is why the same back is fine on a good morning and awful on a bad afternoon. Nothing changed in the back between breakfast and teatime. The alarm setting changed.

Second, there is a gate. In 1965 two researchers called Melzack and Wall showed that signals from the body pass through a gate in the spinal cord before they reach the brain, and that this gate can open wider or close.

  • Movement, touch and warmth close it. Those signals travel on faster nerve fibres and crowd out the pain signals. This is why you rub a sore spot without being told to, why a hot water bottle helps, and why gentle movement usually beats lying still.
  • Poor sleep, stress and worry open it. None of those are your fault, and all of them are worth attention.

3. What the scans show

If you have had a scan, or you are thinking about asking for one, this section matters.

Researchers scanned thousands of people who had no back pain at all. Then they counted what showed up.

  • At 40, about 7 in 10 had disc wear, and half had a disc bulge.
  • At 50, about 8 in 10 had disc wear, and 6 in 10 had a bulge.
  • At 60, nearly 9 in 10 had disc wear, and 7 in 10 had a bulge.

Not one of those people had any back pain.

These findings are like grey hair. Extremely common, they increase with age, and on their own they tell you very little about who hurts.

This is why UK guidance says not to routinely scan back pain outside a specialist setting. It is not about cost. It is that scans are very poor at explaining ordinary back pain, and the words on the report do harm of their own. Degeneration. Wear. Bulge. Frightening words raise threat, threat opens the gate, and people who believe their back is crumbling move it less and guard it more.

Scans earn their place when something specific is suspected that would change the plan. Section 13 lists those. Reassurance is not one of them, because scans are bad at providing it.

4. What actually happens next

Here are the real numbers, from researchers who pooled the good studies and tracked people over a year.

If your back pain is recent, and by recent I mean within about six weeks:

  • Pain scores dropped from around 56 out of 100 to around 26 by six weeks. That is the steep part of the curve, and it happens whatever anybody does. By a year it was around 21.

Read that again, because it is the most useful fact in this guide. Most of the recovery happens early, and it happens on its own.

If you have had it for months, I will be straight with you, because you deserve better than false comfort. In that group the same researchers found pain stayed high and improved only slowly, from around 56 to around 40 over a full year.

That is not a reason to despair. It is a reason to do something active rather than wait. Waiting works for a recent back. It works poorly for a long standing one. The rest of this guide is about what actually helps that second group, and none of it involves resting more.

5. Why it comes in spells

If your back keeps going, that probably feels like failure, or like something is being missed.

It is neither. It is what this problem does.

Researchers followed people who had genuinely recovered from a bout of back pain and watched what happened next.

Within twelve months, 69 in 100 had another bout. About 40 in 100 had one that limited what they could do.

More than two-thirds. In people who had fully recovered.

Let that take some pressure off. Recurring bouts do not mean your treatment failed, or that you are damaging yourself, or that you are heading downhill.

What the same research found about who recurs more is the useful part. Three things stood out: spending a lot of time in awkward positions, sitting more than five hours a day, and having had more than two previous bouts.

Two of those three are about how much of your day you spend still.

So the goal changes. Not "never have a flare again", which nobody gets. Instead: flares that are shorter, further apart, less frightening, and that cost you less. That is realistic, and it is worth aiming at.

6. Guarding: the protection that outstays its welcome

This is the mechanism that keeps ordinary back pain going, and almost nobody has it explained to them.

When your back hurts, you tighten around it. You hold the muscles of your low back and your tummy on, all the time, so nothing moves too far. It is automatic. You did not choose it.

Researchers have measured this. They put people with long lasting back pain on a treadmill and recorded the muscle activity. Compared with people without back pain, the muscles down the spine and across the front of the abdomen were working harder the whole way through the walk. They called it what it is: guarding.

For a week, guarding is sensible. Beyond that it costs you.

  • Muscles that never switch off get tired and sore. True of any muscle. Hold your arm out for ten minutes and see.
  • A back held stiff stops sharing the load, so movement gets concentrated instead of spread.
  • A stiff back gets few gate-closing signals. Remember section 2.
  • The tightness itself starts to hurt. Which then feels like proof that something is wrong, so you tighten more.

That last loop is the trap. Pain leads to guarding, guarding leads to pain, and the pain seems to justify the guarding.

Breaking it does not take force. It takes ordinary, repeated, unthreatening movement.

7. Moving is safe, and resting is the risk

The instinct is to rest until it settles. It is the wrong instinct, and this is well settled in the research.

Bed rest makes back pain worse and makes it last longer. Not neutral. Worse.

What to actually do:

  • Keep doing normal life as far as pain allows. Adapt tasks rather than cancelling them.
  • Move often, in small amounts. Your back responds to frequency more than to effort.
  • Do not wait to be pain free before moving. You will wait a long time, and the waiting is the problem.
  • Judge it by tomorrow, not by today. More ache during or just after is fine and expected. The question is whether you are back to your usual baseline within about a day.
  • Change position often. No position is bad. Staying in any one of them for hours is.

The rule is not "no pain". It is "no lasting increase".

8. Walking, the single best habit

If you take one practical thing from this guide, take this.

A large trial took people who had recently recovered from back pain. Half were given a walking programme, building towards five walks a week of at least thirty minutes. The other half carried on as usual.

The walking group went a median of 208 days before their next bout. The other group went 112 days. Close to twice as long.

No equipment, no gym, no cost.

How to build it:

  • Start from where you actually are, not where you think you should be.
  • Short and often beats long and occasional. Three walks of ten minutes does more for a back than one of thirty, and it is easier to keep up.
  • Add about one tenth a week. Ten minutes becomes eleven. Slower than you want, faster than it feels.
  • Count the total, not the trip.
  • Keep going on the ordinary days. The habit is what works, not any single walk.

9. What turns it up and down

Things that turn the volume up

  • Poor sleep. The strongest one and the most ignored. A bad night makes tomorrow hurt more, reliably.
  • Stress and worry. Not weakness. Stress hormones genuinely make nerves more excitable.
  • Sitting still for long stretches.
  • Fear of what the pain means. Believing your back is fragile makes the alarm louder, and it is not true.
  • Doing far too much on a good day.

Things that turn it down

  • Regular movement spread through the day, rather than one big effort.
  • Sorting your sleep, even partly.
  • Warmth. A hot water bottle is section 2 in action.
  • Company and things you enjoy. Distraction closes the gate, and there is real science behind that.
  • Understanding what is going on. Which is most of why this guide exists.

10. Pacing and flare-ups

The boom and bust pattern: a good day arrives, you feel nearly normal, so you do everything you have been putting off. You pay for three days. Then you rest, feel better, and do it again.

Each cycle teaches your nervous system that activity is dangerous. It is not. It was the size of the jump.

Pacing means deciding in advance, not by feel. On a good day, do the planned amount and stop. It is dull and it works.

When a flare comes, and it will:

  • Do not go to bed.
  • Keep moving, smaller. Shorten the walk. Do not cancel it.
  • Do not hunt for what you did wrong. Often there is nothing. Flares follow a poor week of sleep or a stressful fortnight as often as they follow lifting anything.
  • Expect days to a couple of weeks, then expect it to settle, because it has every time so far.
  • Do not let the guarding outlast the flare. That is the real risk, not the pain.

Every previous flare has settled. That is evidence, and it is yours.

11. A word about painkillers

UK guidance on back pain is firmer than most people expect.

  • Paracetamol on its own is not recommended for back pain. It does not work well for this.
  • Anti-inflammatory tablets can be considered, at the lowest dose that helps, for the shortest time, taking your own risks into account.
  • Strong painkillers are not recommended at all for long standing back pain. Weak ones only for a recent bout, and only if anti-inflammatories are unsuitable or have not worked.
  • Antidepressants and anti-epileptic medicines are not recommended for back pain.

Anti-inflammatories are not free. If you have stomach trouble, kidney problems, high blood pressure, asthma, or take anything for your heart, ask your pharmacist first. You do not need an appointment for that.

Timing beats dose. Use pain relief to unlock a walk you would otherwise skip. Painkillers that help you move are doing a job. Painkillers that help you sit still more comfortably are not.

12. What treatment does, and what it does not

I would rather be straight with you.

What hands-on treatment does well

  • Eases pain in the shorter term, which buys you room to move
  • Reduces the guarding in section 6, which is hard to release on your own
  • Restores movement to areas that have stiffened up in protection
  • Gives your nervous system clear evidence that your back can be moved and touched without disaster

What it does not do

  • It does not put anything back in place. Nothing is out of place.
  • It does not correct your spine. Your spine does not need correcting.
  • It is not a course you complete and then you are fixed.

UK guidance is specific on this point, and I agree with it: manual therapy should be offered as part of a package that includes exercise, not on its own. The treatment opens a window. What you do in that window is what changes the year ahead.

You should expect me to be clear about what I am treating, to change the plan if it is not working, and to tell you when you no longer need me.

13. When to get urgent help

Almost all back pain is not an emergency. A few things are. They are rare, and I am listing them so you can stop wondering.

Go straight to A&E, or call 999

Do not wait to see if these settle. If you are offered a routine appointment, say you are worried about cauda equina syndrome and ask to be seen today.

  • Numbness or a changed feeling between your legs, around your back passage or genitals. Including toilet paper feeling different when you wipe.
  • Trouble starting to wee. Not being able to feel it coming out. Losing the feeling of a full bladder. Any new loss of control of your bladder or bowels.
  • New numbness during sex, or new trouble getting an erection.
  • Numbness or pain down both legs at once.
  • New or quickly worsening weakness in one or both legs, or your legs giving way.
  • Sudden severe pain in your back or tummy with faintness, a cold sweat, or a throbbing feeling in your tummy.
  • Severe pain straight after a bad fall or accident.

Contact your GP or NHS 111 today

  • Weakness that is getting worse, especially if your foot is starting to drag or catch.
  • Sudden severe back pain after a minor fall, a bump, a cough or a sneeze, or with no cause at all. Especially with osteoporosis, long term steroid use, or a previous broken bone from a minor injury. This can be a spinal fracture. It is common, treatable, and often missed.
  • Back pain with a fever, shivering, or feeling generally unwell.
  • Back pain with weight loss you cannot explain, night sweats, or a past history of any cancer.
  • Pain that is much worse at night, wakes you every night, and is not eased by any change of position.

Worth mentioning at a normal appointment

  • Whether it is improving, even slowly. Direction matters more than level.
  • What makes it better and what makes it worse. The most useful thing you can bring.
  • Any new symptom in your legs.

Everything else, including a bad flare-up, can wait for a normal appointment.
