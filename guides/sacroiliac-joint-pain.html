Understanding Your Sacroiliac Joint Pain

Pain at the back of the pelvis, how pain works, and what the guidelines say

Written by Tom Wikeley, chiropractor, registered with the General Chiropractic Council (No. 05268). Last reviewed November 2026.

Please read this first

This is one of the guides I hand to my own patients after I have examined them. I have put them on the website so they are easy to find and re-read, and so anyone can learn how pain works. They are general information, not a diagnosis, and not a treatment plan for anybody I have not assessed.

No guide can tell you what is causing your pain. That takes a proper history and examination. If nobody has examined you, please treat this as background reading and see your GP, a chiropractor, a physiotherapist or another qualified clinician about your own symptoms. If a clinician who has examined you has given you different advice, follow theirs, not this.

Some symptoms need urgent care, not reading

Call 999 or go straight to A&E if you have any of these, whatever else this guide says. Do not wait to see if they settle.

  • Numbness around your bottom, genitals or inner thighs
  • Problems passing urine, or losing control of your bladder or bowels
  • Weakness or numbness in both legs

Section 11 has the full list, including what to take to your GP or NHS 111.

Your pain sits low down at the back of your pelvis, near the dimple above your buttock. It may spread into your buttock, your groin or the back of your thigh. It is often worse with standing on one leg, stairs, turning in bed, or getting in and out of the car.

This is called sacroiliac joint pain. It is a common type of low back pain, and most people get better without needing anything more than the steps in this guide.

You may have been told your pelvis is "out", "twisted" or "unstable". This guide explains why that is not true, how pain really works, and what the current guidelines recommend.

The short version

  • The sacroiliac joints are some of the strongest joints in your body. They move only a tiny amount.
  • Your pelvis is not out of place. It does not slip out, and it does not need putting back in.
  • Pain is an alarm, not a damage meter. A sensitive joint can hurt a lot without being damaged.
  • Scans are not usually helpful for this kind of pain. A careful examination tells us more.
  • Current guidelines recommend starting with simple care: advice, staying active, exercise, and hands-on treatment alongside it.
  • Injections and surgery are only for a small number of people whose pain lasts many months despite good care. Section 7.
  • Most people improve. It usually takes weeks to a few months, with ups and downs along the way.

1. What and where the sacroiliac joint is

At the base of your spine is a triangle-shaped bone called the sacrum. On each side of it sit the two large wing-shaped bones of the pelvis, called the ilia.

Where the sacrum meets each ilium is a sacroiliac joint. You have two, one on each side. They sit under the dimples just above your buttocks.

Their job is to pass the weight of your upper body down into your legs, and to absorb force when you walk, run and land.

These joints are built for strength, not movement:

  • The joint surfaces have ridges and grooves that lock together, a bit like two pieces of a jigsaw.
  • They are wrapped in some of the thickest, strongest ligaments in the body.
  • Big muscles cross them, including the buttock muscles and the deep muscles of the low back.

The nerve supply comes from small nerve branches leaving the bottom of the spine, at the levels called L5 and S1 to S3. These same levels also supply the buttock, the groin and the back of the thigh. That is why pain from the joint is often felt in those areas too. It rarely goes below the knee.

2. How common it is, and who gets it

The sacroiliac joint is the main source of pain in roughly 15 to 30 in every 100 people with long-lasting low back pain. It usually sits low, below the belt line.

It is more common:

  • During and after pregnancy, when hormones loosen the ligaments and the pelvis takes extra load
  • After a fall onto the buttock, or a jolt such as missing a step
  • After an operation to fuse the low back, because the joints below take on more work
  • With repeated twisting, lifting or one-sided loading, such as running, some sports or some jobs

Often there is no clear cause at all. That is normal, and it does not change how well it gets better.

3. Your pelvis is not out of place

This is the most important thing in this guide, because the myths cause a lot of worry.

Research using very precise X-ray measurements shows these joints move only a tiny amount. That is a couple of degrees of turning, and around a millimetre of sliding. They cannot slip out of place in everyday life. It takes a major accident, such as a serious car crash, to move one significantly.

In one study, people had their sacroiliac joints measured before and after manipulation. Their pain improved, but the position of the joint had not changed at all.

So if treatment makes you feel better, it is because it has calmed the sensitivity in the area, not because something has been put back.

Words like "out", "twisted" or "unstable" make people frightened to move. Fear makes the body guard and tense, which usually makes the pain worse. Your joints are strong, and moving them is safe.

4. Pain is an alarm, not a damage meter

How pain works

Your body has danger sensors in your joints, ligaments and muscles. When tissue is stretched, squashed or irritated, they send warning signals to your spinal cord and then to your brain.

A warning signal is not pain. Your brain weighs it up against everything else it knows. What were you doing? What happened last time? How tired or stressed are you? Do you feel safe?

Then it decides how much protection you need. Pain is the result.

The gate in your spinal cord

In 1965, two scientists described a gate in the spinal cord. It controls how much of the warning signal reaches your brain.

  • Movement, warmth, touch and feeling calm help close the gate.
  • Stress, poor sleep, worry and fear open it wider.

Your brain can also send signals down to change the gate. That is why the same joint can feel fine one day and very sore the next.

When the alarm gets sensitive

When pain lasts for weeks or months, the alarm system gets better at its job. It becomes more sensitive. It starts going off with smaller and smaller triggers, like a car alarm that goes off when a cat walks past.

This does not mean more damage. It means the alarm needs resetting. Because it learned to be sensitive, it can learn to settle down again.

Hurt does not mean harm

Some discomfort when you move is normal while things settle. A good rule:

  • Pain that rises a little during an activity and settles back within about 24 hours is fine.
  • Pain that is still worse the next day means you did a bit too much. Do a little less next time. You have not caused harm.

5. Things that turn your pain up and down

Pain is affected by much more than the joint itself.

Things that often turn pain up

  • Poor sleep
  • Stress, from work, home, money or caring for others
  • Worry about what the pain means
  • Staying still for long spells, or avoiding movement
  • Doing too much on a good day, then paying for it
  • Frightening words, such as "out of place" or "unstable"

Things that often turn pain down

  • Moving little and often
  • Good sleep and a regular routine
  • Understanding what is going on. Reading this counts.
  • Warmth, gentle touch and hands-on treatment
  • Doing things you enjoy, and time with people you like

6. How it is diagnosed, and why scans rarely help

Sacroiliac joint pain is diagnosed with a careful history and examination.

  • You often point to one spot, just next to the dimple at the back of the pelvis.
  • Pain is often worse with standing on one leg, stairs, rolling in bed, and getting out of a car.
  • I use a group of tests that press on and stretch the joint. When three or more of them bring on your usual pain, the sacroiliac joint is a likely source. Research shows this group of tests is much more reliable than any single test.
  • I also check your low back, your hips and your nerves, because problems there can feel similar.

Scans such as X-rays and MRI cannot reliably tell a painful sacroiliac joint from a pain-free one. The international guidelines published in 2025 do not recommend routine scans for this kind of pain.

Scans are useful when something else is suspected, such as an inflammatory condition, a fracture, or an infection. Section 11 lists the signs that would point that way.

7. What the current guidelines say

In 2025, an international group of specialists published the first detailed guidelines on sacroiliac joint pain. They were endorsed by 21 medical organisations. In England, NICE guidance on low back pain also applies.

The guidelines describe a step by step approach. Most people only ever need step one.

Step one: simple care, for everyone

  • Advice and reassurance about what is going on
  • Staying active, and returning to normal activities gradually
  • Exercise, including strengthening the muscles around the pelvis, hips and trunk
  • Hands-on treatment, as part of a package that includes exercise
  • A pelvic belt, which some people find helps for a while
  • Pain medicine for a short time if needed, as advised by your GP or pharmacist

An honest note: most of the research on this simple care comes from studies of low back pain in general, not from studies of the sacroiliac joint alone. But it is safe, it helps most people, and it is what every guideline recommends first.

Step two: specialist options, for pain that does not settle

For a small number of people, pain carries on for months despite good care. A pain specialist may then consider:

  • A numbing injection into or around the joint, done using X-ray or ultrasound guidance. This helps confirm whether the joint is the source. A steroid may be added, but relief usually lasts weeks rather than months.
  • Radiofrequency treatment. This uses heat to quieten the small nerves that carry signals from the back of the joint. Trials comparing it with a dummy procedure show it can help selected people, often for six months or more. It is only offered if a test injection first gives good relief.

Step three: surgery, for very few people

Surgery to fuse the sacroiliac joint is only considered after at least six months of good non-surgical care has not worked, and when injection tests have confirmed the joint is the source. NICE allows a minimally invasive version of this operation in carefully selected people. It is uncommon, and the guidelines rate the evidence for it as weak.

What the guidelines do not recommend

  • Routine scans for ordinary sacroiliac joint pain
  • Long-term use of strong painkillers such as opioids
  • Injections without X-ray or ultrasound guidance

8. What you can do now

Keep moving

  • Walk little and often. Several short walks are kinder than one long one while things are sore.
  • Change position regularly. Get up and move every 30 to 45 minutes.
  • Keep doing your normal activities, adjusting them for a while rather than stopping.

Small changes that ease the load

  • Stand with your weight on both feet. Try not to hang on one hip.
  • Get in and out of the car with both legs together. Sit down first, then swing both legs in.
  • Take stairs one at a time if they are sore, using the handrail.
  • Sleep with a pillow between your knees if you lie on your side.
  • When dressing, sit down to put on socks, shoes and trousers.

Pace yourself

  • On a good day, do a little more, not a lot more. Big jumps often lead to flares.
  • Break big jobs into smaller pieces, with rests in between.

These are not forever rules. As things settle, you will do all of this normally again.

9. Flare-ups

Flare-ups are normal. They do not mean you have damaged anything, or that you are back to square one.

  • Keep moving gently. Shorter walks, more often.
  • Use heat on the area if it helps.
  • Use the small changes in section 8 more strictly for a few days.
  • Rest in short spells, not whole days.
  • Build back up once it settles, from where you dropped to.

Most flares settle within a few days to two weeks.

10. What treatment does, and what it does not

Hands-on treatment can ease pain and muscle tension for a while. It closes the gate in the spinal cord and gives you a window where moving feels easier. That is a real effect, and it is worth having.

What it does not do is put your pelvis back in place. Nothing is out of place, and any clicks you hear are just gas moving within a joint.

Guidelines recommend hands-on treatment alongside exercise and activity, not on its own. The lasting change comes from gradually building strength and confidence. My job is to settle things when they flare, guide you through the rest, and keep you on track.

11. When to get help

Call 999 or go to A&E straight away

  • Numbness around your bottom, genitals or inner thighs
  • Problems passing urine, or losing control of your bladder or bowels
  • Weakness or numbness in both legs

Contact your GP or NHS 111

  • Fever, feeling very unwell, or a hot, red, swollen area
  • Unexplained weight loss, night sweats, or a past history of cancer
  • Severe pain after a fall, especially if you have thin bones or take steroid tablets
  • Pain or numbness spreading below the knee, or a foot that drags
  • If you are pregnant, mention pelvic pain to your midwife, who can arrange specialist help

Talk to your GP at your next appointment

These can be signs of an inflammatory condition called axial spondyloarthritis, which needs blood tests and a specialist:

  • Back or buttock pain that started before the age of 45 and has lasted more than 3 months
  • Stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes every morning
  • Pain that wakes you in the second half of the night, and eases when you move
  • Buttock pain that swaps from side to side
  • Psoriasis, Crohn's disease, colitis, a painful red eye, or a family history of these

Come back and see me

  • A flare that has not settled after two weeks
  • Not improving after six to eight weeks

Everything else can wait for your normal appointment.
